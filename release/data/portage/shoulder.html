Sheet I · The Shoulder · c. 450 BCE → 1523

Before the city, the shoulder.

Step I of V

Carrying enters the record as punishment, as love and as intelligence: Atlas under the sky, Aeneas under his father, Protagoras under a well-tied load. It ends this sheet in Venice, with a saint wading the lagoon.

c. 30–15 BCE
01 · Punishment

Atlas, and the women of Caryae.

The oldest carrying figures are punished ones. Atlas holds up the sky because he lost a war (Hesiod, Theogony 517). Vitruvius says the first caryatids were the women of Caryae, carved holding up a roof forever, and the first atlantes the Persian captives of the portico at Sparta (De architectura I.1.5–6).

“… the victors represented them draped, and apparently suffering under the burthen with which they were loaded, to expiate the crime of their native city.”

Vitruvius, De architectura I.1.5, c. 30–15 BCE, tr. Joseph Gwilt, 1826.

Architecture’s bearing figures begin as a penal image, and that history reaches Venice. It is taken up on The Burden, in “Made to carry”.

c. 450 BCE
02 · The thesis

Protagoras, porter.

Philosophy begins with a well-packed load. Protagoras of Abdera earned his living as a porter. Democritus saw how he had tied his bundle of wood and took him as a pupil (Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights V.3).

“… balanced and held together with all but geometrical accuracy.”

Aulus Gellius, Noctes Atticae V.3, c. 180 CE, tr. J. C. Rolfe, 1927.

“He too invented the shoulder-pad on which porters carry their burdens … for he himself had been a porter.”

Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers IX.53, 3rd century CE, tr. R. D. Hicks, 1925, citing Aristotle and Epicurus.

The two Greek words

Gellius says Protagoras was one of the men “whom the Greeks call achthophoroi and we Latins baiuli, or porters”. Garzoni, in 1585, still calls the Venetian porters baiuli in Latin.

The sheet sets the text; the figure that carries his idea, the pad and the tumpline, is below.

100 BCE – 500 CE
03 · The body’s tools

The tumpline.

On the other side of the world a potter made a bottle in the shape of a man under a load, held by a strap across the forehead (Moche, north coast of Peru). The strap is the tumpline: the same band the Mesoamerican tlamemeh called the mecapal and Nepalese porters call the namlo.

The pad, the strap, the frame: few tools, everywhere the same. The variety is in how the body takes the load. The frames and their kilograms are on The Weight.

c. 19 BCE · 1581
04 · Love

Aeneas: the dear weight.

Aeneas carries his father out of burning Troy, and his father carries the household gods. The load is chosen, not imposed: “this labour will not weigh me down” (Virgil, Aeneid II.708).

ergo age, care pater, cervici imponere nostrae;
ipse subibo umeris nec me labor iste gravabit

“Haste, my dear Father, (’tis no time to wait,) / And load my Shoulders with a willing Fraight.”

Virgil, Aeneid II.707–708, c. 19 BCE; English by John Dryden, 1697.

c. 1260 · 1423
05 · The ferryman

Christopher at the ford.

Christopher, the Christo-phoros, “Christ-bearer”, is the giant who carries travellers across a river. The child on his shoulders grows heavy as lead, because he is carrying the world and its maker (The Golden Legend, c. 1260).

“Child, thou hast put me in great peril; thou weighest almost as I had all the world upon me, I might bear no greater burden.”

Jacobus de Voragine, Legenda aurea, c. 1260, “Of S. Christopher”, in William Caxton’s English, 1483.

He is the patron of porters, boatmen and travellers. To see his face was to be spared a bad death that day, which is why he was painted huge at doors and gates. The Buxheim woodcut says so in its own inscription:

Cristofori faciem die quacumque tueris / Illa nempe die morte mala non morieris.

“On whatever day you look at Christopher’s face, on that day you will not die a bad death.” 1423 (tr. here); transcribed, to check against the print.

1523–24
06 · Venice

The Christ-bearer in the lagoon.

Titian painted the giant in 1523–24 over a doorway on the stair to the Doge’s private apartments. The saint wades through the lagoon, with San Marco and the Bacino behind him: the ferryman of the legend as a carrier in Venetian water.

Venice painted Christopher again and again: Bellini at San Giovanni Grisostomo (1513), Pordenone at San Rocco (1527–29), the marble saint over the door of the Madonna dell’Orto, once dedicated to San Cristoforo.

Here the shoulder meets the city. The next sheet is the city’s own system of carrying: licences, places, altars.
