Sheet 8 · The Burden · why carry

Why carry.

Across the five steps

The movements say when, The Map says where, The Weight says how heavy. This sheet asks why: what writers and painters have made of a body under a load, from Homer to the present.

1585
01 · One posture, two readings

Fa chino, fa inchino.

fa chino: he goes bent.
fa inchino: he bows.

Tomaso Garzoni, La piazza universale, Venice, 1585 (tr. here).

Garzoni reports both readings of the word facchino, porter. Some say he is called so “because he goes bent”, fa chino, stooped over his work; the Bergamasques themselves say it is “because he bows”, fa riverenza, & inchino alle persone. Both are folk etymologies: dictionaries call the word’s origin uncertain.

The same posture, read two ways: a body bent by the load, or a body bowing in courtesy. That double reading is the thread of this site.

1776 · 1885
02 · Equal at birth

The philosopher and the street porter.

“The difference between the most dissimilar characters, between a philosopher and a common street porter, for example, seems to arise not so much from nature, as from habit, custom, and education.”

Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776, Book I, ch. II.

Smith adds that a porter “can find employment and subsistence in no other place” than a great town. Venice, a city without wheels, is the porter’s great town. Smith’s pair is old: Protagoras the porter became a philosopher, see The Shoulder.

The porter’s own complaint

“All living things were made of a little drop of sperm, / Thine origin is mine and my provenance is thine; / Yet the difference and distance ’twixt the twain of us are far / As the difference of savour ’twixt vinegar and wine.”

Sindbad the Hammal, in The Book of the Thousand Nights and a Night, Night 538, tr. Richard F. Burton, 1885, vol. 6. Smith’s argument, set as a porter’s verse.

c. 16 BCE · 1580
03 · How it is carried

A load borne well.

Leve fit, quod bene fertur, onus.

“A load borne well becomes light.” Ovid, Amores I.2.10, c. 16 BCE (tr. here).

The question moves from what is carried to how it is carried: the porter’s skill, and the ground of his dignity. The science says the same in its own terms: a trained carrier makes the load part of the swing of the walk (Heglund et al. 1995).

Homer · 1548–49 · 1942
04 · Without end

Sisyphus, carrying.

“… its weight would be too much for him, and the pitiless stone would come thundering down again on to the plain.”

Homer, Odyssey XI.593–600, tr. Samuel Butler, 1900.

In Homer, Sisyphus rolls his stone. Titian, in the Prado’s Sisyphus, has him carry it on his shoulders: the porter’s posture, painted by a Venetian.

1883Nietzsche, the camel“What is heavy? so asketh the load-bearing spirit; then kneeleth it down like the camel, and wanteth to be well laden.” Thus Spake Zarathustra I, tr. Thomas Common
1942Camus“Il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux.” One must imagine Sisyphus happy. Le Mythe de Sisyphe (tr. here)
1947Simone Weil“All the natural movements of the soul are governed by laws analogous to those of physical gravity. Grace alone is the exception.” La pesanteur et la grâce (tr. here)

Mark 15:21 · 1747–49
05 · Compelled, and chosen

Simon of Cyrene.

“And they compel one Simon a Cyrenian, who passed by, coming out of the country, … to bear his cross.”

Mark 15:21, King James Version.

A countryman pressed into carrying: the situation of the Bergamasque porter in Venice. Giandomenico Tiepolo painted Simon in Venetian light for the Via Crucis of San Polo, 1747–49.

Paul holds both ethics three verses apart: “Bear ye one another’s burdens” (Galatians 6:2) and “every man shall bear his own burden” (6:5). Goya painted the first: two workers carrying an injured mason.

c. 30 BCE · 1310 · 1669 · 1881
06 · Made to carry

Made to carry.

Some carrying is not a trade at all. Vitruvius says the bearing figures of architecture began as punishment: the women of Caryae, carved holding up a roof “to expiate the crime of their native city” (I.1.5), and the Persian captives whose statues held up the portico at Sparta (I.1.6).

In the Frari in Venice, Doge Giovanni Pesaro’s monument, designed by Baldassare Longhena and completed in 1669, stands on four colossal male figures in black marble, dressed in white marble, barefoot, their clothes torn at the knees. Each bears the upper storey on a padded load set on the shoulders and neck.

What they represent is contested, and the sheet says so. The broad agreement is that they show Black men as subjugated bearers, a triumphal image of the doge’s power: Vitruvius’s atlas of the conquered, given a racial form. Emeka (2020) reads them as people enslaved in the war over Crete, which Venice lost in 1669, and calls the monument “a racist monument before systematic racism”. Others read them as “Moors” standing for the Ottoman enemy. Fred Wilson set banners of these figures in his US Pavilion, Speak of Me as I Am, at La Biennale di Venezia in 2003.

Rodin’s Fallen Caryatid has collapsed under her block and goes on holding it.

e qual più pazïenza avea ne li atti, / piangendo parea dicer: ‘Più non posso’.

“And he who had most patience in his looks / Weeping did seem to say, ‘I can no more!’”

Dante, Purgatorio X.130–139, c. 1310–15, tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867. Dante’s figure “makes of the unreal real anguish” in the one who sees it.

Majjhima Nikāya 22
07 · Putting it down

The raft.

The Buddha tells of a man who crosses a river on a raft he has made, and then asks:

“Why don’t I, having hoisted it on my head or carrying it on my back, go wherever I like?”

Alagaddupama Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya 22, tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu.

The answer is that the wise man leaves the raft on the shore once it has carried him over. Part of the meaning of carrying is knowing when to put the load down.

1966 – 2018
08 · Artists who carry

Named, not shown.

These works are in copyright, so the sheet names them and does not show them.

William Kentridge

The Head & the Load, 2018: a procession-performance about the African carriers of the First World War, around a million of whom worked for the armies.

Michelangelo Pistoletto

Scultura da passeggio, 1966–67: a ball of compressed newspaper rolled by hand through the streets of Turin, a sculpture that exists only while it is moved.

Francis Alÿs

Paradox of Praxis 1, Mexico City, 1997: a block of ice pushed through the streets for about nine hours until it has melted. Its subtitle: “Sometimes making something leads to nothing”.

Also on the theme: Richard Serra’s One Ton Prop (House of Cards), 1969, four lead plates that stand only by leaning on one another; Mierle Laderman Ukeles’s Manifesto for Maintenance Art, 1969.
