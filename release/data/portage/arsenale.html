Sheet 11 · The Arsenale · 1220 → 2026

A yard of carried things.

The walk ends inside Venice’s shipyard. For five centuries the Arsenale turned timber, hemp and iron into galleys by moving them from hand to hand. Its men carried the Doge, and its own shed, Tesa 41, was built in 1834 for coopers and oar-makers. What it was, how it worked, and what it carried.

1220 → 1797
01 · The yard

Arzanà.

The name first appears in 1220, in a grant of the lake of San Daniele, “partim in arsena”. The lake passed to the Comune in 1325 for the Arsenale Nuovo. Inside its walls the Republic built, stored and repaired its fleet until 1797.

Chi fa suo legno novo e chi ristoppa …
altri fa remi e altri volge sarte

One builds his ship new, one caulks … another makes oars, another twists rope.

Dante, Inferno XXI, 7–15: the Venetians’ arzanà in winter (tr. here).

Twenty lines later a devil runs up the crag with a sinner on his shoulder: “L’omero suo, ch’era aguto e superbo, / carcava un peccator con ambo l’anche”, his sharp, proud shoulder loaded with a sinner by both haunches. The first written picture of the Arsenale’s divided work is followed at once by a body carried on a shoulder.

c. 1436 → 1574
02 · The line

From one window the cordage.

“From the windows they handed out to them, from one the cordage, from another the bread, from another the arms … ten galleys, fully armed, between the hours of three and nine.”

Pero Tafur, in Venice about 1436, on galleys towed past the Arsenale’s storehouses (tr. Letts, 1926).

In 1494 the Milanese canon Pietro Casola saw “a great crowd of masters and workmen who do nothing but build galleys”, twelve smiths each at his own forge, “many women who do nothing but make sails”, and a ropewalk “so long that I could hardly see from one end to the other” (tr. Newett, 1907).

1494Casola, on the wagesAt least 1,200 ducats paid out every Saturday, the heads of the Arsenale told him.
24 July 1574Henri III, King of France“In un’ora fu posta insieme e armata di tutto punto una galea”: in one hour a galley was put together and fully armed, before his eyes the accounts · the hour.
The same dayA servant from Montagnana“Tutti li lochi separati che si lavorano una cosa istessa”: every place separate, each working one same thing.

The collation laid for the king was all sugar: when he took up his napkin it broke in two. The salute guns killed two men. Each retelling makes the galley faster or bigger: two hours in one, a cannon cast and a galley rigged while he dined in another. The servant, who had slipped in with a gentleman’s party, ended the day “stracho et stuffo” and locked in: the man with the keys had gone to lunch.

1545 → 1602
03 · Not in series

The wood will obey the mould.

The Arsenale is often called the first assembly line, with interchangeable parts. It worked in sequence, with divided trades and stock models, but its parts were not identical. Each galley had a number, and its fittings carried the same number, “proprio perché non sono fatti in serie”, because they were not made in series (Lazzarini 2021, after Concina).

Masts, yards, oars, rudders and sails were made to models fixed by the Senate, within narrow limits. Some fitted a group of galleys built to one mould.

Il legno obedirà al sesto et non il sesto al legno.

The wood will obey the mould, and not the mould the wood.

Baldissera Drachio, master shipwright, 1590s, proposing one mould for every galley (tr. here).

His reforms met such resistance in the yard that he fled the Arsenale and came back only eight years later. Every master shipwright kept his own mould.

8 Aug 1545The Senate orders a reserve of 100 light galleys, kept in their sheds. It needs 16,000–18,000 oars in store.

1602109, “numero e qualità che alcun tempo mai Vostra Serenità ha avuto”. The target seems to have been met only then.

Before the war of Cyprus (1570) two galleasses were widened at the outriggers and no longer fitted through the water gate. The towers on both sides had to be shaved back. The building was cut to let its own work out.

1463 → 1740s
04 · From forest to slip

Spesa de’ facchini.

Everything the yard used arrived carried. Galley masts came from the Somadida forest in Cadore, reserved in 1463. Its villages owed “uomini, buoi e carri”, men, oxen and carts, to haul trunks 25–30 m long to Perarolo on the Piave, where raftsmen floated them to Venice.

Big oaks from the Montello were sawn where they fell into two or three pieces “per facilitarne la condotta”, so they could be carried. Beech for oars came from the Cansiglio. Carrying it by land cost six times as much as by water. Of 2,100 oars ordered in 1637, 1,756 arrived; the rest were broken, unfit, or “stolen or abandoned on the way”. In 1628 “a woodcutter died under a tree”.

In Istria in the 1740s a commissioner had 6,342 oaks felled “nel sottil della luna di febbraio”, in the thin of February’s moon, and persuaded the villagers to haul 2,322 abandoned trunks to the shore themselves. Bargemen carrying timber for the Arsenale were paid partly in oaks.

1631A galleass, costedThe hull’s labour line reads “spesa de’ marangoni, calafai, facchini”: carpenters, caulkers, porters. Porters were a cost of every hull.

1592 → 1638
05 · The body as the measure

Under its own heavy weight.

“To avoid the danger of the ship parting under its own heavy weight, a danger to which small boats are not subject.”

An old foreman of the Arsenale, in the opening of Galileo’s Two New Sciences, 1638 (tr. Crew and de Salvio, 1914).

From that remark Galileo builds his science of the strength of materials: a larger structure of the same shape is not equally strong, and may break under its own weight. The first modern science of load starts from a workman talking about a body carrying itself.

In 1592–93 the Provveditore Giacomo Contarini polled the foremen, the veterans and Galileo on how to make the great galleys faster. Galileo treated the oar as a lever. Contarini answered within a week that he had left out “the limited human force of the oarsmen”. The head of the oar-makers warned that a longer oar would break and “the handle will strike him in the breast”. Another said a thicker oar could be worked by “neither only 4 men nor even six” (Renn and Valleriani, 2001, from the archives).

1437 → 1645
06 · The arsenalotti

Two in every dozen.

From 1437 the caulkers’ guild made every foreman who hired five masters also take a veteran, an old or disabled caulker, at the same pay: “do per dosena”. By 1486 young men maimed in brawls were claiming it, and the guild asked to keep it for men over sixty.

Apprentices started under fourteen and served seven years. Masters could not teach the craft to foreigners or slaves, and shipwrights and caulkers were forbidden to leave Venice. Coryat, in 1608: the workmen grown “decrepit and unable to worke any longer, are maintained in the same at the charge of the citie during their lives”.

Casola in 1494 and the servant from Montagnana in 1574 both saw women making sails. Later 25–40 velere cut sails inside the yard under a forewoman.

c. 1594Moryson: “some foure hundred Artificers” at work daily
1590On the books: 200 ship carpenters, 450 caulkers, 100 oar-makers
1608Coryat: 1,500 men, 2,000 crowns a week
1645Evelyn: “ordinarily 500”
“16,000”The figure often quoted probably comes from a doge’s deathbed speech of 1423, a census of the whole city’s trades, not the Arsenale’s payroll. Not used here.

The yard gave its men bevanda, watered wine, over two litres a day in the 1600s. From the 1630s it ran from a fountain; before that it was “lugged in buckets” round the yard. At the Ducal Palace fires of 1574 and 1577 some arsenalotti “si fecero legar con una corda et calar dove era il fuogo”, had themselves tied to a rope and let down into the fire, and refused any reward. In 1569 a riot over the loss of Saturday-afternoon pay came just before the powder store blew up: 20 dead, heard in Padua.

1610 → c. 1779
07 · Carried by the Arsenale

Portato da gl’humeni dell’Arsenal.

A new Doge was carried round the Piazza by the men of the Arsenale. Franco’s plate of 1610 says it under the picture: he “entra in un Palchetto con suoi Parenti et l’Armiraglio, dove portato da gl’humeni dell’Arsen[al]”, he climbs into a box with his family and the Admiral, carried by the men of the Arsenale.

“The Carriers did not wear the red cap until Francesco Morosini first gave it to them, in 1688.”

Giovanni Grevembroch, text for his watercolour of the Doge in the pozzetto, 18th century (tr. here).

Gabriel Bella’s painting of about 1779 has the same scene, with a cartouche: “Il Doge Viene Porttato Intorno La Piazza Di S. Marco Spargendo Dinari”, the Doge is carried round the Piazza scattering money. In Franco’s labelled procession every emblem of the state has its bearer: the sword, the umbrella, the cushion, and the Doge’s chair on one man’s shoulder. The state’s ceremony is a chain of carried things.

1610 · 1645
08 · The gate at evening

Qui si paga la maestranza.

Franco’s plate of the land gate carries its own text: “questa gente che si vede è la maestranza, la quale entra la mattina ed esce fuori la sera con bellissimo ordine”, these people are the workforce, who go in in the morning and come out in the evening in the finest order.

At a barred window on the left: “Qui si Paga La Maestranza”, here the workforce is paid. A man takes his money through the grille. Evelyn saw it in 1645: the workmen “every evening receive their pay thro’ a small hole in the gate”.

In front, men come down the steps with blocks and bundles of wood on their shoulders and heads. Workers took scrap wood home as firewood, the stelle; where custom ended and theft began was hard to say. The Arsenale’s picture for Portage is not a ship but this evening stream of carriers.

The lions at the gate came from Piraeus as booty in 1687. Goethe found them so huge “that all around appears little, and man himself would become as nought”.

1557 → 2026
09 · Tesa 41

Barrels and oars.

The pavilion stands where the Cistercian nuns of the Celestia had their garden until 1806–10. The canal in front of it was dug out of that garden in 1557–64 as the Arsenale’s timber pond.

In 1834 two sheds “for the production of barrels and oars” were built on the open ground by the canal, to the design of the engineer Giovanni Casoni, copying the old tese. The Combatti plan of 1846 draws two equal halls on that spot, no. 7, and its key names them “Officine dei bottai e remai”, the coopers’ and oar-makers’ workshops. That the Navy’s Magazzino n. 41 is one of them is near-certain; the register that would prove it has not been found. Which hall made barrels and which made oars is not recorded, nor when the oars stopped.

Casoni’s own guide of 1829 describes the old oar workshop, where the Great Council sat after the Ducal Palace fires (he prints 1674–77, a misprint for 1574–77), and real sweeps of 42–50 Venetian feet, 14.6–17.4 m.

1786 → 1918
10 · After the Republic

An old family.

Goethe, on 5 October 1786: the Arsenale “has an appearance like that of an old family, which still bustles about, although its best time of blossom and fruit has passed”.

The Republic fell on 12 May 1797. On 9 January 1798 French soldiers hacked the gilded carving off the last Bucintoro, piled it on San Giorgio Maggiore and burned it to collect the gold from the ashes. The hull became a gun battery, then a prison hulk, and was broken up in 1824. A master caulker who had rowed the Bucintoro under three Doges remembered the Ascension dinner he did not get that year.

Then the Navy: steam corvettes fitted out in the old basins, a dry dock, guns in the war of 1915–18. The Biennale came later, and in 2024 the sheds by the Canale delle Galeazze were opened to the public.
