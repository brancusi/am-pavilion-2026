Sheet III · The World · 1801 → 1946

The porter everywhere.

Step III of V

After the Republic, the porter is seen everywhere, and framed in a few ways: as a beast of burden, a licensed citizen, a hero of labour, a bearer of honour, a union man. This sheet takes them one at a time.

1801 · 1857
01 · The beast of burden

Going on a man’s back.

In 1801 Humboldt crossed the Quindío pass in the Andes, where travellers were carried in a chair strapped to a man’s back.

“They talk in this country of going on a man’s back (andar en carguero), as we mention going on horseback.”

Alexander von Humboldt, Researches concerning the institutions and monuments of the ancient inhabitants of America, English ed. 1814, vol. 1, pp. 66–70.

He adds that “no humiliating idea is annexed to the trade”, and that when a road for mules was proposed, the cargueros “presented formal remonstrances against mending the road”. In the Mexican mines, he writes, men “are called his horses (cavallitos), because they are saddled every morning”.

1857Holton, New Granada“the cargueros (as human beasts of burden are called)”; the heaviest load carried to Bogotá by one carguero “is said to have been carried by a woman. It is given at 216 pounds”
1814the loada carguero carried a traveller seated in a bamboo chair, held by straps across the chest

1861 · 1901
02 · The licensed citizen

A number, a badge, a test.

In the cities the porter was licensed. The forts des Halles, the porters of the Paris central market, wore a numbered brass plate and a wide leaded hat. To be admitted, a candidate carried 200 kg over 60 m. That an entrance test existed is documented: L’Illustration drew it on 9 November 1901.

1861London, the ticket portersthey “have exclusive right of all porterage by hire from ‘aliens or foreigners’ … They usually, but not always, wear white aprons, and display their tickets as badges” (Mayhew)
1861London, the Porters’ Resta shoulder-height bench on Piccadilly, “for the benefit of porters and others carrying burdens” (plaque)
c. 1900Vienna, the Dienstmannthe licensed, numbered street porter

1875 · 1896 · 1923
03 · The hamal

Constantinople.

The Istanbul porter, the hamal, carries on a stuffed back-saddle, the semer. The word is the Arabic hammāl; Genoa’s camallo comes from the same root.

In 1885 Richard Burton glossed Sindbad the Hammal as “a porter like the famous Armenians of Constantinople”.

In Constantinople, Armenians were known as porters. In the 1896 massacre they were among the dead, and a British witness wrote that afterwards “the reliable Armenian hamals (porters) were no more”.

Burton 1885, “the famous Armenians of Constantinople”; George Young, Constantinople, London 1926, p. 225, quoted in S. Dinçer, TSEG 10.4, 2013, pp. 20–45. Kurdish porters took their places at the Custom House (Dinçer).

1870 – 1885
04 · The hero of labour

Like a saint.

Painters made the carrier a monument. Repin spent summers with the barge haulers of the Volga; of Kanin, the man at the head of the line, he wrote that he “appeared none the less as a man of dignity; he was like a saint”.

Constantin Meunier cast the Antwerp dock porter in bronze, Le Débardeur, and made the dock porter a public monument. Dostoevsky praised Repin’s painting because it does not lecture the viewer.

1897 · 2025
05 · Honour

Paying to carry.

The same act becomes an honour when the load is a saint. At Gubbio, in 1897:

“Those Ceraioli who will actually carry the burden are free of the table, but those who are only to hold the steadying guy-ropes must pay five lire for the privilege.”

Herbert M. Bower, The Elevation and Procession of the Ceri at Gubbio, 1897.

1925 · 1946
06 · The union

The Brotherhood, and the Compagnia Unica.

Organised porters won contracts. In 1925 A. Philip Randolph founded the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters; in 1937 it won the first major contract signed by a Black-led union. Passengers had called every Pullman porter “George”.

In Genoa the Caravana, the company that since 1340 had admitted only men from Bergamo, lost its Bergamasque monopoly in the 19th century. In 1946 the port’s companies merged into the Compagnia Unica, the CULMV, which still works the port.

1895 · 1908 · 2006
07 · Still carrying

Tea, couches, head-pans.

On the road from Sichuan to Tibet, tea porters carried bricks of tea, resting standing up on a T-shaped staff. De Rosthorn, in 1895, judged “11 or 12 packages (250 to 280 lbs.) to be the usual quantity”.

Nepalese porters carry up to 183 % of their own body mass (Minetti et al. 2006), with a basket, the doko, and a strap across the forehead, the namlo. In Accra and Kumasi the kayayei, migrant girls and women from the north, carry loads in head-pans; kaya is Hausa for load, yoo Ga for woman. The high-altitude Sherpa and Bhotia porters of the Everest expeditions were called the “Tigers”.
